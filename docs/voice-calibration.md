# Voice Calibration

The operational companion to `public-voice.md`. That file says what Sepehr sounds like in principle;
this one records how his voice was corrected in practice, with the rules extracted from his own edits.

---

## The test

> Don't try to make Sepehr sound like a designer.
> Make him sound like a smart designer explaining something to another person.

Read any sentence and ask: **would he say this out loud, to someone across a table?**
If it only works written down, it's wrong.

His strongest public voice is **not designer manifesto**. It's clear observations that happen to
reveal a design philosophy. An observation is attached to something concrete. A manifesto floats free.

---

## Approved lines

These three were written, corrected by Sepehr, and approved. They are the reference for tone.

1. **Recognition** (his strongest — closest to his natural voice)
   > A mark should be recognisable before anyone reads the name. That's what I work back from, whatever the format.

2. **System**
   > Making one good piece is easy. The harder part is building a system that makes the next one just as strong.

3. **Subtraction** (best client reassurance, weakest as a headline)
   > Most briefs start with too much. The work is figuring out what actually matters, then removing everything that gets in the way.

## Rules extracted from his edits

**Order by process, not by rhythm.** Never save the strongest word for the end of the sentence.
Say things in the order the work actually happens.
- ✗ "deciding what to cut so the thing that matters survives" (arranged for cadence)
- ✓ "figuring out what actually matters, then removing everything that gets in the way" (the real sequence)

**Keep the small inefficiencies.** Speech optimises for connection, writing optimises for economy.
Words like *actually*, *that's what*, *just as* are what make it sound spoken. Copywriting deletes them.
- ✗ "I work back from that"
- ✓ "That's what I work back from"

**No invented numbers for vividness.** Concretion-for-effect is the loudest copywriting tell.
- ✗ "the next fifty" · ✓ "the next one"

**Phrasal and plain verbs over single elegant ones.**
- ✗ arrive with / deciding / determine · ✓ start with / figuring out / work out

**Name the activity, not the artifact.**
- ✗ "One good piece is easy" · ✓ "Making one good piece is easy"

**Avoid consultant nouns.**
- ✗ "the harder problem" · ✓ "the harder part"

**Never anchor a general claim to one medium.** His work spans identity, editorial, social, packaging,
film and AI. Any sentence built on "a poster…" contradicts half the portfolio. Claims should describe
**method**, not artifact.

## Structural consequence for the site

The largest type slots (`statement-title`, `big-copy`) are manifesto-shaped by design: one line,
enormous, nothing around it. That format fights the observational voice.

**So the display slots take the least voice, not the most** — plain and factual. The real voice belongs
in `body-copy` and the case studies, where an observation has something to attach to.

Currently violating this: **"Direction is not decoration."** on About — pure manifesto, to be replaced.

## Audience

Both commercial (shops, agencies, e-commerce) and cultural (film, editorial, music). The copy therefore
can't lean on either vocabulary. This is consistent with the voice rather than a compromise: "a smart
person explaining something" reads correctly to a shop owner *and* a director.
