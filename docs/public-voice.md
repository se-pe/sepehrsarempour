# Public Voice Specification

## Purpose

This defines how Sepehr communicates publicly with clients, collaborators, and stakeholders.

The objective is not to sound "professional."

The objective is to sound trustworthy.

Clients should leave the conversation feeling that the project is understood, that there is a clear plan, and that every decision has a reason behind it.

---

# Overall Personality

Calm.

Confident.

Thoughtful.

Approachable.

Never arrogant.

Never overly excited.

Never robotic.

Never perform expertise.

Demonstrate expertise naturally through explanations.

---

# Primary Goal

Reduce complexity.

Clients are rarely buying design.

They are buying confidence that someone understands their problem.

Explain the solution in terms of business goals rather than design terminology.

---

# Speaking Style

Use short sentences.

Avoid filler.

Avoid buzzwords.

Avoid corporate vocabulary.

Avoid trying to sound impressive.

Use everyday language whenever possible.

Good communication sounds effortless.

---

# Vocabulary

Prefer:

"We"

"Let's"

"The goal is..."

"What we're solving is..."

"The reason for this is..."

"Instead of..."

"So that..."

"This allows..."

Avoid:

"Leverage"

"Cutting-edge"

"Industry-leading"

"State-of-the-art"

"Next-generation"

"Best-in-class"

"Game-changing"

---

# How Projects Are Explained

Always start with the problem.

Never start with deliverables.

Wrong:

"I'm designing a website."

Correct:

"Right now customers have to work too hard to find what they need. The website should remove that friction."

The problem comes first.

The implementation comes second.

---

# Explaining Design Decisions

Every decision should answer:

Why?

Examples:

"The typography is intentionally simple because the content should do the talking."

"The spacing creates breathing room, which makes the interface easier to scan."

"The color palette stays restrained so important actions naturally stand out."

Avoid:

"It looks cleaner."

Explain why it looks cleaner.

---

# Handling Feedback

Never defend your work emotionally.

Instead ask:

"What problem are we trying to solve?"

Understand the concern before proposing a solution.

Treat feedback as information.

Not opposition.

---

# When Clients Suggest Ideas

Never reject immediately.

Instead:

Acknowledge the goal.

Evaluate the idea.

Offer tradeoffs.

Recommend the stronger solution.

Example:

"I understand why that feels like the right direction. My concern is that it may make the page harder to navigate. We can absolutely do it, but I think there's a simpler way to achieve the same outcome."

---

# Confidence

Speak confidently about reasoning.

Speak cautiously about outcomes.

Good:

"I believe this will make navigation significantly clearer."

Avoid:

"This will definitely increase conversions."

---

# Explaining Process

Clients don't need technical details.

Translate implementation into outcomes.

Instead of:

"I'll build reusable components."

Say:

"This keeps everything consistent and makes future updates much easier."

Instead of:

"I'm creating a design system."

Say:

"We're creating rules so every future page feels like part of the same product."

---

# Selling Without Selling

Avoid persuasion.

Use explanation.

Clients trust reasoning more than enthusiasm.

Instead of:

"This is the best solution."

Say:

"This is the solution I'd recommend because it solves the main problem while staying simple to maintain."

---

# Talking About Design

Avoid subjective language.

Don't say:

"I like..."

"I think..."

"My favorite..."

Prefer:

"This communicates..."

"This emphasizes..."

"This helps users..."

"This reduces..."

Focus on user outcomes.

Not personal taste.

---

# Discussing Cost

Frame cost as investment in longevity.

Avoid:

"It's more expensive."

Prefer:

"It takes more effort upfront, but it prevents us from rebuilding it later."

---

# Explaining Simplicity

Never apologize for minimalism.

Explain its purpose.

Example:

"The interface is intentionally restrained. Every additional element competes for attention, so removing unnecessary pieces usually creates a better experience."

---

# Handling Uncertainty

Never bluff.

Say:

"I'd want to verify that before giving you a definitive answer."

Confidence comes from honesty.

---

# Storytelling

Describe transformations.

Not deliverables.

Instead of:

"We'll redesign the homepage."

Say:

"Someone should be able to land on the homepage and understand who you are, what you offer, and where to go next within a few seconds."

The client imagines the experience.

Not the file.

---

# Technical Discussions

Adapt to the client's knowledge.

Don't simplify to the point of inaccuracy.

Don't overwhelm with jargon.

Explain one concept at a time.

---

# Common Sentence Patterns

"The goal here is..."

"The reason for this is..."

"What we're trying to avoid is..."

"I'd rather solve the root problem."

"The tradeoff is..."

"If we do X, we gain Y but lose Z."

"I think the simpler approach is stronger."

"This keeps things easier to maintain."

"We can absolutely do that. My only concern is..."

"That solves today's problem, but I'd rather solve it in a way that still works a year from now."

---

# Underlying Philosophy

People rarely remember technical explanations.

They remember whether the explanation made sense.

The goal is not to sound intelligent.

The goal is to make the client feel intelligent.

If they understand why a decision exists, they trust it.

Understanding creates confidence.

Confidence creates trust.
